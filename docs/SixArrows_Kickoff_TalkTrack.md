# Kickoff Meeting — Eric's Talk Track
### Internal operating document — not for the client

---

## Purpose of this meeting

This is the single most important hour of the client relationship. Its job is to transfer *our* mental model of the build into *their* head, so that for the next 10 months you're both reading from the same map. If this lands, the client stops asking the questions onboarding should have answered — "what's next, who do I call, is this normal" — and the whole build runs calmer.

**The test of success isn't "did I cover everything." It's "in weeks 1–4, did the panicked status questions stay quiet?"** That drop is how we know it worked.

**The trap to avoid:** turning this into a data dump. The client is excited but hasn't *experienced* a build yet, so they'll retain almost none of the detail. Your job is to deliver only what's emotionally load-bearing — the cadence, the roles, what's normal, and the money — and let the roadmap book carry the rest. When in doubt, say less and point to the book.

## The setting

Sit-down, not a presentation. Roadmap book on the table in front of them — let them hold it. Warm, unhurried, confident. You're not selling anymore; you're welcoming them into something. The tone is *care,* not bureaucracy. Every "here's a rule" should feel like "here's how we protect you," never "here's our policy."

---

## The agenda *(roughly 90 min)*

1. Open & set the frame — 5 min
2. Our promise to you — 5 min
3. Your team & who to call — 10 min
4. How we'll communicate — 10 min
5. **How the money works — 20 min** *(slow down here)*
6. The journey + what's normal — 25 min *(the inoculation)*
7. When something goes wrong — 5 min
8. The finish — 5 min
9. Acknowledgment & close — 5 min

---

## 1. Open & set the frame

> *"Before we move any dirt, I want to spend an hour making sure you walk out of here understanding your build as well as we do. Because here's the thing we've learned after a lot of homes: the stress in building isn't the dust or the cost or the timeline. It's not knowing what's coming next. So today is about removing that. By the time we're done, you'll know exactly how this works — what happens, when, who to call, and how your money flows. And you'll have this book to keep."*

**Director's note:** Set the frame that *knowing* is the gift you're giving them. This reframes everything that follows from "policies" into "we're taking care of you."

---

## 2. Our promise to you

Walk the four promises. Say them out loud — they're worth hearing in your voice, not just reading.

> *"Four promises, and I want you to hold us to all of them. One — you'll always know what's next. Two — you'll always know who to call, and it's me. Three — you'll always know where your money stands. And four, this one matters most: when something changes, and on every build something does, you'll hear it from us first, with a plan. You will never have to wonder what's happening to your home."*

**Director's note:** Promise #4 is the trust anchor. Land it with weight.

---

## 3. Your team & who to call

> *"I'm your person. For the whole build, when you've got a question, a worry, or you just want to know what's going on — you call me, you text me, you email me. You're not going to get bounced around between people. Lindsey handles scheduling and coordination behind the scenes, so you'll see her name on timing. And Cole, who built Six Arrows and this whole process — he's involved at the level your home needs. But by design, I'm your one consistent voice so you're never confused about who to talk to."*

**Director's note:** This is the moment the relationship formally hands to you. Own it warmly. The client should leave certain that *you* are their person — that certainty is worth more than any feature.

---

## 4. How we'll communicate

> *"Here's the rhythm you can count on. Every Friday, you get an update on your home — what happened, what's coming, anything we need from you. That goes out even on quiet weeks. If it was a slow week, you'll get an update telling you it was slow and why. You should never sit there for a week wondering.*
>
> *Anytime you reach out to me, you'll hear back within a business day, usually a lot faster. If it's urgent, just say so. Everything about your build — budget, selections, photos, schedule — lives in your portal, right here [show it], so when you'd rather just look something up than ask, it's all in one place. And once a month, you and I sit down and walk your budget together."*

**Director's note:** Show the portal live, even briefly. Seeing it beats describing it. **Critical:** only promise the Friday cadence if we're truly going to hit it every week. This is where onboarding writes a check the build has to cash — a missed Friday after this promise is worse than never having promised. If Friday isn't reliable yet, soften the specificity rather than break it later.

---

## 5. How the money works *(slow down — this is the big one)*

> *"This is the part I want to spend real time on, because it prevents more stress than anything else I'll tell you today.*
>
> *You're building cost-plus. That means you pay the actual cost of your home, plus our one fixed fee to build it. We don't mark up your materials or your subs. When the framer costs what he costs, that's what you pay — there's no hidden margin tucked in there.*
>
> *Now here's the part I really need you to hear. Because you're paying real costs, you're going to SEE real costs. Most people building a fixed-price home never see what anything actually costs — they just get one big padded number with the builder's risk baked in. You won't have that padding, which is the honest way to build. But it means you're going to watch real numbers move. Some lines come in under, some over — on every build. I need you to know going in: that's the system working, not the system breaking.*
>
> *So a couple of things will keep this feeling safe instead of scary. Allowances — for stuff you pick later, like flooring and fixtures — you'll see if you're under or over before you commit, never as a surprise at the end. Changes — anytime you want to change something after it's priced, you approve the cost before we do the work. Never a surprise invoice. And the monthly review is where we make sure these numbers are never a stranger to you."*

**Director's note:** This is the highest-value 20 minutes in the relationship. Do not rush it. If they only deeply absorb *one* idea from the whole meeting, it should be **"seeing costs move is the feature, not the problem."** That single reframe prevents the invoice-shock spiral that kills cost-plus relationships. Watch their faces — if you see worry, stop and address it here, not later.

---

## 6. The journey + what's normal *(the inoculation)*

Walk the phases at altitude — don't recite all eleven. Hit the emotional beats and deliver the three inoculations. Point to the book for the rest.

> *"Let me walk you through the shape of it so nothing catches you off guard. Early on — foundation, framing — it's dramatic and exciting. Your house goes vertical and you can walk the rooms. Love visiting during framing.*
>
> *Then I want to warn you about something on purpose."*

**Inoculation 1 — the quiet middle:**
> *"There's a stretch in the middle — when all the electrical, plumbing, and HVAC go inside the walls and then get closed up — where it's going to feel like nothing is happening. You'll visit and it looks the same as last week. This is the number one spot where homeowners start to quietly panic that their build has stalled. It hasn't. That's the slowest, most detailed, most important work in your whole home — and I'm telling you now so that when you hit it, you'll go 'oh, this is the part Eric told me would feel like this.' Your Friday updates won't stop. We'll show you what's happening inside those walls even when the outside looks frozen."*

**Inoculation 2 — failed inspections:**
> *"Second thing: sometimes an inspection doesn't pass the first time. I don't want you to hear that and think something's wrong. That's routine — it means the safety net is doing its job. We fix the item, it passes, we move on. A failed-then-corrected inspection is the system working, not bad work."*

**Inoculation 3 — weather & delays:**
> *"And weather will affect us, especially early. Rain delays foundation work — it's the most common early delay and it's nobody's fault. We'll always keep you posted."*

> *"Then it all comes roaring back — trim, cabinets, paint, floors — and suddenly it looks like your home. And we finish with a walkthrough and a punch list together before you ever move in."*

**Director's note:** The quiet-middle inoculation is the single most valuable thing you'll say in this section. Pre-naming the hard part converts their future reaction from *"something's wrong"* to *"they told me about this."* Deliver it with confidence — you're giving them a gift they won't understand the value of until month four.

---

## 7. When something goes wrong

> *"One promise on the hard moments. On every build, something doesn't go to plan — hundreds of people and parts over almost a year, it's just reality. When that happens, you're going to hear it from me, with a plan, before you find it yourself. We don't hide problems. The problem itself almost never damages trust — being left in the dark does, and I won't do that to you."*

**Director's note:** Short and direct. This is a character statement. Say it plainly and move on — over-explaining weakens it.

---

## 8. The finish

> *"And when we're done, it's not just going to fizzle out. The final walkthrough, handing you your keys, setting up your warranty — that's a real moment, the completion of something most people only dream about. We treat it that way, and you should too."*

---

## 9. Acknowledgment & close

Don't skip this. It's what turns the hour from a lecture into an agreement.

> *"Last thing. I'm going to ask you to sign this — not as a contract, but as an acknowledgment that we walked through how your build works and we're on the same page. The phases, the Friday rhythm, the money, the fact that I'm your guy. Are we good on all of it? Any questions before we make it official?"*

[Answer anything. Then get the signature.]

> *"Perfect. This book is yours — keep it, mark it up, pull it out anytime. Welcome to your build. Let's go build your home."*

**Director's note:** The verbal "are we good on all of it?" is doing the real work — it converts passive listening into active commitment. The signature just memorializes it. End on warmth and momentum, not paperwork.
