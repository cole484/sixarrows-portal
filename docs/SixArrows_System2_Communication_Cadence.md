# Communication Cadence System
### System 2 — The heartbeat

*Internal operating system. The load-bearing system: onboarding writes the biggest check against this one.*

---

## Why this is the make-or-break

Of all eight systems, this is the spine. The single biggest destroyer of trust in a build is the black hole — the client reaches out, then hears nothing for three days and fills the silence with anxiety. The cure isn't more communication. It's *rhythm* — a fixed heartbeat the client can set their watch by.

And here's the trap that makes this system fragile: **a cadence that fires 60% of the time is worse than no cadence at all.** Once onboarding promised "every Friday," a missed Friday isn't a neutral non-event — it's a broken promise landing on your highest-trust moment. So this system has two jobs, and the first matters more than the second:

1. **Reliability** — it fires every single week, without depending on anyone's memory or free time.
2. **Value** — each update actually informs and reassures.

A reliable empty update beats a brilliant sporadic one. Design for the streak first.

---

## Part 1 — Reliability mechanics (the part that actually matters)

### Fixed day, every week
Pick one day and never move it. **Default: Friday.** Friday closes the week with a sense of progress and gives the client the weekend to absorb it without needing a response. The fixed day does double duty: the client *expects* it (predictability), and its absence becomes conspicuous — which creates the accountability pressure that protects the streak.

### Low friction = high reliability
The number one reason updates slip is that they're a blank-page chore. Solve it with a **pre-filled template** (Part 2) so producing the update is fill-in-the-blanks, not writing. Target: **under 10 minutes** to produce. If it takes 45, it will slip. If it takes 8, it gets done.

### A trigger that isn't a human memory
- **Thursday:** recurring, non-negotiable calendar block for Eric — "draft this week's updates."
- **Friday AM:** recurring block — "send/post updates."
- The template is already waiting in each project, half-filled from the schedule and the week's photos.

### A backstop so the streak never breaks
If Eric is out or slammed, **Lindsey produces the minimal version** — even just "quiet week, here's where we are, here's what's next" with a photo. The streak is sacred. Better a three-line update than a broken Friday.
- **Friday noon rule:** if an update isn't posted, Lindsey pings Eric or posts the minimal version herself.

### The quiet-week rule
**The update fires even when nothing dramatic happened — *especially* then.** Quiet weeks are exactly when clients start to worry, and exactly when builders skip updates. A quiet week gets an update that *says* it was quiet and why: "Slow week by design — we're mid-rough-in, which is detailed and not very visible. Here's what's happening inside the walls. Back to visible progress around [date]." This is the inoculation content from onboarding, paying off in real time.

### Where it lives
Posted to the **portal** (single source of truth) + a short heads-up so they know it's there: *"Your weekly update is posted."* The portal holds the record; the nudge drives them to it.

### Track the streak
A simple count: **updates delivered on time, per project.** This is both an accountability mechanism and a CX metric that rolls into Cole's monthly review. If a project's streak breaks, that's the early warning that this client's experience is degrading — before they tell you in a survey.

## Part 2 — The update template

Short, skimmable, same shape every week so the client learns where to look. Five sections, each answering a question already in their head:

> **[Project] — Week of [date]**
>
> **1. This week** *(→ progress; attach 1–3 photos)*
> What got done. Plain and concrete. *"Framing crew completed the second floor and started the roof structure. Plumbing rough-in began in the basement."*
>
> **2. What's ahead** *(→ predictability)*
> What to expect next week. *"Roof structure wraps up, then we start dry-in — windows are scheduled to arrive Tuesday."*
>
> **3. Schedule status** *(→ predictability / slip notification)*
> On track, or moved and why. *"On track for dry-in by [date]."* — or — *"Windows pushed three days by the supplier; we've adjusted and your dry-in milestone moves from the 12th to the 15th. No downstream impact."*
>
> **4. Anything from you** *(→ feeling in control)*
> Upcoming decisions or action items — or explicitly *"Nothing needed from you this week."* Never leave this blank; the blank reads as "did I miss something?" *"Heads up: tile selections are due by [date] — Ben will reach out this week to walk you through options."*
>
> **5. Notes** *(optional)*
> A budget note, or a Tier 2 item from the exception system. *"One small thing: the electrician found the panel location needed a minor shift — already handled, no cost or schedule impact."*
>
> — Eric

### Quiet-week variant
Sections 1–2 get shorter and honest; Section 3 reaffirms on-track; Section 4 still fires; the quiet-week explanation lives in Section 1. The point is the client never confuses *quiet* with *stalled.*

### Voice
Warm, confident, plain. Eric's voice, signed by Eric, replies go to Eric — every update reinforces that **he is their one person.** No corporate tone, no jargon. Write like a competent friend who happens to be building your house.

## The weekly update is where the systems become visible

Worth seeing clearly: the heartbeat is the **integration surface** where the other systems surface to the client each week.

- **Photos** (System 6) → Section 1
- **Schedule status / slips** (System 5) → Section 3
- **Upcoming decisions** (System 3) → Section 4
- **Tier 2 exceptions** (System 7) → Section 5
- **Budget notes** (System 4) → Section 5

That's why it's load-bearing. It's not one more channel — it's the weekly moment all the work you're doing becomes *felt* by the client. Do this well and the rest of the systems get credit for existing.

---

## Response SLA (the other half of cadence)

The weekly push handles proactive contact. The reactive side: **when the client reaches out, they hear back within one business day**, usually faster. Stated at onboarding, honored here. Urgent flagged items get same-day. This closes the black hole from both directions — they never wait on the rhythm, and they never wait on a reply.
