# Exception Handling System
### System 7 — When something goes wrong

*Internal operating protocol. Serves the Trust pillar above all others.*

---

## The principle

Every build has problems. Hundreds of people, materials, and decisions over the better part of a year — something always goes sideways. **The customer experience is not decided by whether problems happen. It's decided by how they're handled.**

The problem itself rarely damages trust. Being kept in the dark does. A delay revealed at the walkthrough is a betrayal; the same delay surfaced 30 days out with a plan is competence on display. This system exists to make sure every exception travels the second path, every time, without depending on anyone's mood or memory in the moment.

One promise governs all of it: **the client hears it from us, with a plan, first.**

---

## What counts as an exception

Anything that threatens one of the three customer expectations — **time, cost, or quality** — or deviates materially from the plan. Common forms:

- A sub flags, no-shows, or can't perform *(this is the handoff from the work order reconfirm loop)*
- A discovered condition — rock in excavation, rot or a surprise behind demo, a site issue
- A material delay or backorder on a long-lead item
- A failed inspection requiring rework beyond the routine
- A cost surprise or an allowance trending materially over
- A quality miss caught internally before the client sees it
- Weather or an event beyond the normal, expected range

**Not everything is a client-facing exception.** Over-reporting every hiccup erodes the signal exactly as much as hiding real problems erodes trust. The triage below is how you tell the difference.

> **A note on how onboarding shrinks this load:** the things we *pre-named* in the kickoff — weather delays, routine failed-then-corrected inspections, the quiet middle — are no longer surprises. When they happen, they're handled in normal cadence, not escalated. Good onboarding converts tomorrow's "exception" into today's "expected." This system is for the surprises we *couldn't* pre-name.

---

## Triage — the three tiers

The owner classifies every exception the moment it's caught. This is the core of the system: it resolves the tension between *don't bury the client in noise* and *never hide a real problem.*

### Tier 1 — Absorb
No impact the client would notice on time, cost, or quality. Handled internally, **logged, not escalated.**
*Examples: a sub sends a different qualified crew member; a minor internal resequencing that doesn't move a milestone.*

### Tier 2 — Inform (in cadence)
Real but contained impact — a milestone moves a few days, an allowance trends over, a routine inspection re-do. The client is told **through the normal weekly update**, with the fix already in motion. Proactive, not alarming.
*Frame: "Here's a small thing that came up and here's how it's already handled."*

### Tier 3 — Escalate now (direct contact)
Material impact to budget, schedule, or quality — **or anything emotionally significant to the client** regardless of dollar size. The client is contacted **directly and promptly**, not at the next Friday update. Situation + plan + options.
*Examples: a discovered condition adding real cost; a significant delay; a quality issue requiring rework; anything touching meaningful money.*

> **Money rule: any single cost surprise above $1,000** is automatically Tier 3 — communicated directly, before any related work proceeds. Below $1,000, the variance still flows to the live portal budget (nothing is ever silently absorbed) and gets a mention in the weekly update if notable (Tier 2). And regardless of amount, anything touching a client hot-button is Tier 3. This keeps exception handling and the change-order process aligned.

## The response loop

Six steps. Every exception, every time.

### 1. CATCH
Anyone can raise an exception — sub, site crew, Eric, Lindsey, an inspector. **Lower the bar to surfacing.** A culture where problems get raised early is worth more than any document. Nobody is ever in trouble for flagging something fast.

### 2. OWN
The exception immediately gets **a single owner** so it doesn't float. Default owner: **Eric.** A problem without a name attached decays — same rule as the systems themselves.

### 3. ASSESS & TRIAGE
The owner determines the tier (impact on time/cost/quality + emotional weight) and what's actually known versus still unknown.

### 4. FORM THE PLAN — *the cardinal step*
**Never communicate a naked problem.** A problem always travels with at least a next step and a timeline.

This does *not* mean waiting until you have a perfect, complete solution — that delays the news and risks the client discovering it first. It means pairing the problem with forward motion:

> *Wrong (naked problem):* "We hit rock during excavation, not sure what it'll cost."
> *Wrong (sitting on it):* says nothing for four days while assembling a full price.
> *Right (problem + next step + timeline):* "We hit rock during excavation. Here's what that can mean for cost and schedule, here's exactly what I'm doing to get you precise numbers, and I'll have those to you by Thursday."

### 5. COMMUNICATE *(per tier)*
Tier 1 → log only. Tier 2 → weekly update. Tier 3 → direct, prompt contact. Eric is the single client-facing voice (consistent with the onboarding handoff). See scripts below.

### 6. RESOLVE & CLOSE THE LOOP
When it's handled, **tell the client it's handled.** "That rock issue from last week — resolved, here's where we landed." Closing the loop is what converts a problem into a deposit in the trust account. An unclosed loop leaves a quiet worry running.

Then **LOG** it. Every exception goes into the issue log → over time this becomes the lessons-learned database that surfaces patterns (the thing that keeps going wrong) across projects.

## How to communicate a problem — the craft

This is where trust is won or lost. Six elements:

1. **You first** — before they could ever discover it themselves.
2. **Plan attached** — never a naked problem (Step 4).
3. **Straight** — no minimizing, no spin, no burying it in qualifiers. Clients smell a soft-pedal.
4. **Own it cleanly** — if it was our miss, say so plainly and move to the fix. No groveling, no defensiveness.
5. **Options where they exist** — giving the client a choice restores their *feeling of control*, which is the pillar a problem threatens most.
6. **Confirm when resolved** — close the loop.

> **The distinction Eric will face constantly:** some exceptions are nobody's fault (rock, backorder, weather), some are our miss (missed an order, a sub's poor work, a sequencing error). The framing differs slightly — discovered conditions get "this is the kind of thing a custom build surfaces, here's how we handle it"; our mistakes get clean ownership. But the protocol is identical, and here's the key: **a mistake handled well can build more trust than no mistake at all.** Clients don't expect perfection. They expect honesty and competence under pressure. This system is how you show both.

### Tier 3 script (direct contact)
> *"[Name], I want you to hear this from me right away. [What happened, straight.] Here's what it means for [time/cost/quality]: [plain assessment]. Here's what we're already doing about it: [plan]. [Where options exist:] You've got a couple of ways we can go — [A or B] — and I'll walk you through the tradeoffs. I'll have [next concrete update] to you by [date]."*

### Tier 2 line (in the weekly update)
> *"One thing that came up this week: [item]. We've already [action taken], so [milestone/budget] stays on track / shifts by [small amount]. Nothing you need to do — just keeping you in the loop."*

## Cardinal rules

1. The client never discovers a problem first.
2. Never communicate a problem without a next step and a timeline.
3. Never minimize, spin, or hide — the cover-up always costs more than the problem.
4. Every exception has exactly one owner.
5. Catching is everyone's job; communicating is the owner's job (one voice: Eric).
6. Close every loop — tell them when it's resolved.

## How it connects to the rest of the system

- **Work order reconfirm loop →** a sub flag is an incoming exception; it enters here at Catch.
- **System 5 (schedule) →** schedule-impacting exceptions trigger the slip-notification protocol.
- **System 4 (financial) →** cost exceptions align with the change-order process (the Tier 3 money rule).
- **Lessons-learned database →** the log feeds pattern detection over projects, so recurring problems get designed out, not just handled again.
